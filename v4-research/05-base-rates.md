Fetched 2026-09-24. Every rate below is tagged: [measured] = platform or study data with a stated n; [vendor claim] = a vendor number with no n or with a self-serving definition; [analyst estimate] = a synthesis or assumption; [unverified] = could not fetch the primary page, not used as a fact.

Key definitional warning: every cold email "reply rate" below counts any reply. Woodpecker's own definition: "any response: positive interest, a polite decline, a question, even an out-of-office message". No fetched primary source publishes a separate "positive reply" rate. Instantly's 2026 report: positive reply rate "Not separately reported". Belkins: "Not separately specified". Treat "close rate" as applied to all replies, which flatters cold email.

## 6. Interpreting zero replies: the rule of three

Hanley and Lippman-Hand, JAMA, April 1, 1983, Vol 249, No. 13, pp 1743-1745 (McGill reprint PDF): "This 'rule of three' states that if none of n patients shows the event about which we are concerned, we can be 95% confident that the chance of this event is at most three in n (ie, 3/n). In other words, the upper 95% confidence limit of a 0/n rate is approximately 3/n. (This approximation is remarkably good: when n is larger than 30, the rule of three agrees" closely with the exact value. Derivation given: "exp(–n × maximum risk) = 0.05."

Eypasch, Lefering, Kum, Troidl, BMJ 1995 Sep 2;311(7005):619-620 (PMC2550668): "upper limit of 95% confidence interval = maximum risk = 3/n (for n > 30)".

Applied to outreach. Zero paying clients after n touches tells you only that p <= 3/n at 95% confidence:

| n touches, 0 conversions | 95% upper bound on p (3/n) | exact 1 - 0.05^(1/n) |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | 15.0% | 13.9% |
| 50 | 6.0% | 5.8% |
| 100 | 3.0% | 2.95% |
| 160 | 1.9% | 1.85% |
| 480 | 0.63% | 0.62% |

So 160 cold sends with zero clients cannot distinguish p=1.4% (the WhiteHERO assumption) from p=0.05% (Belkins-level); it is consistent with both. Bayesian version with a uniform prior (Beta(1,1)): after n zeros the posterior is Beta(1, n+1), mean 1/(n+2), 95% upper bound 1 - 0.05^(1/(n+1)); for n=160 that is 1.84%, essentially the same as the rule of three. Corollary: to learn that p < 0.5% you need 600 zero-conversion touches; a solo sender cannot learn cold-email base rates from their own data within a quarter.

## 7. Touches needed for a 50% and 90% chance of at least one paying client

Model: each touch converts independently with probability p. P(at least one in N) = 1 - (1-p)^N. Solve N = ln(1-q) / ln(1-p) with ln(0.5) = -0.6931 and ln(0.1) = -2.3026. For small p, N50 = 0.693/p and N90 = 2.303/p.

Worked example, p = 0.30% (3% reply x 10% close): N50 = -0.6931 / ln(0.997) = -0.6931 / -0.003005 = 230.7; N90 = -2.3026 / -0.003005 = 766.4.

Check on the existing plan (160 sends/month, 480 in 90 days):
- At the plan's own p = 1.4%: P(>=1 in 480) = 1 - 0.986^480 = 99.9%, and 1 - 0.986^160 = 89.5% in the first month. The plan's stated 13% figure is not consistent with its own inputs; either it applies further unstated funnel losses or the 5.6%/25% inputs are not what it actually uses.
- At p = 0.343% (platform average any-reply x 10% close): P(>=1 in 480) = 1 - 0.99657^480 = 80.8%.
- At p = 0.10% (1% x 10%): 38.1%.
- At p = 0.045% (Belkins strict cold x 10%): 19.4%.
The realistic band for a new domain with no sender history and a developer-audience is the lower half: roughly 20 to 40% chance of one paying client from 480 cold sends, and any-reply counts include declines, so the true close-on-reply is below 10%.

Comparison at equal effort: 10 warm referral touches at p=4.8 to 7.2% give a 39 to 53% chance of a client; 10 cold emails at p=0.1 to 0.45% give 1 to 4%. The per-touch multiplier is 15x to 70x; the constraint on the warm route is supply of introducers, not conversion. Upwork proposals at 6% win sit between the two, and unlike referrals their supply is not constrained, but the 6 to 20% range is a vendor claim for established profiles.
